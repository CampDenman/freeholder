# Changelog

All notable changes to Freeholder are recorded as changesets and summarized
here. The heading version is the same string `package.json`, health, OpenAPI
and the SDK publish.

## 0.1.0

Active development, not a release candidate.

### Major Changes

- License Freeholder-authored code, documentation, deploy tooling, SDKs, and templates under Apache License 2.0. Package manifests and distributable license files now use `Apache-2.0`, source headers are consistent across the monorepo, and the licensing gate verifies all three surfaces. Previously released AGPL-3.0-only core and MIT package versions keep their original terms; Apache-2.0 applies from this change forward. Bundled third-party assets continue to retain their own license notices.

### Minor Changes

- What someone may use is now a grant, not a flag on the page. A membership, a class pass, a one-time unlock, a loyalty tier or a decision you make by hand all grant the same kind of access, and the site asks that question at the moment of looking rather than trusting a leftover checkbox. A membership starts a site-wide grant for the period already paid for. Pausing it takes the access away; cancelling at period end leaves it until the period runs out. Member-only products hide from strangers and show to whoever holds a grant. You can also grant and revoke access by hand from Access in admin.
- You can now hand your bookkeeper a file their software will actually take. Under Reports → Accounting exports, define an export, pick whether it is for QuickBooks, for Xero, or a plain spreadsheet, and choose whether it goes out every week, month or quarter — or only when you press the button. Whoever you name gets an email with the figures and a link; the file itself is never attached, because it lists every customer and what they paid, and an inbox is not a safe place for that. The list shows the one thing that matters about a scheduled file: whether the mail provider accepted every recipient's copy. A later bounce changes that answer back to failed. A failed delivery says so in red, keeps the file so you can send it by hand, and waits for an explicit retry instead of mailing a bad address every hour. An export that should have gone out and did not is flagged at the top of the page rather than quietly not happening. Every recipient gets a separate private link that expires after 30 days. The raw token exists only inside the encrypted mail outbox and the email; Freeholder stores its HMAC and allows the download only after a delivering provider has accepted that recipient's message. Two things it deliberately will not do. It will not add two currencies together — one export covers one currency, and any invoices in another are left out of the file and counted separately, so a reconciliation that comes up short always tells you why. And it will not keep your books: there is no chart of accounts here, so the account code, tax code and product code go in once, from your bookkeeper, and get copied onto every row without Freeholder having an opinion about them. Refunds are reported beside the total rather than turned into credit notes for you.
- Your ad positions now actually show ads. Upload the artwork, give it a click-through and a description, and it runs in the positions you sold it into — at the size those positions declare, on a laptop and on a phone, from one placement. A text-only sponsor line is there too, for a newsletter or a blog where a banner would look wrong. Space nobody has bought falls back to your own promotion, if you asked it to. Your own promotions need nobody's approval but yours; anything somebody paid for has to be looked at before it appears, and goes back for another look the moment its artwork or its destination is edited. Every ad is labelled as one, on the page, and there is no setting that turns that off. Visitors leave through your own link rather than the advertiser's, so a click is counted and the destination cannot quietly be swapped for a different one after you approved it. A link that has been tampered with goes nowhere. And when it is time to bill, a campaign raises an ordinary draft invoice against the advertiser's contact — the same invoice, tax and reporting path as anything else you sell. You issue it from the invoice, where the tax question is asked properly. One thing to redo if you were an early adopter: an ad position could previously carry its own house image and link, set on the position itself in the editor. That setting has gone, because it was a second, unlabelled kind of ad that none of your reports could see. If you had set one, create it as a house campaign instead and it will fill the same space — labelled and counted this time.
- Sell advertising on your own site, if that is your business. Define the positions where ads appear, place them on pages like any other block, and the space is reserved at every screen size — so an ad arriving late never shoves the article down the page. An advertiser is a contact like everybody else, so the local bakery buying a banner and the local bakery buying prints is one person in your records, not two. Campaigns carry their own dates, rates, budgets and priority, and a paid one cannot go live until it has been approved. Your own house promotions skip that, because they are yours already. Line items say what runs where: which positions, which pages, which countries and devices, which days and hours, and how often one person should see it.
- Ads now count first-party. An impression is recorded when the visible creative renders; a viewable impression after half of it has been on screen for one continuous second. Daily totals, unique visitors, clicks and spend roll up for the advertiser report. Even pacing and frequency caps come from those numbers. A per-thousand or per-click campaign can be reconciled against what actually delivered, on the same invoice.
- Third-party ad tags are supported and off by default. An HTML tag or a named network creative only runs when the slot allows it and the visitor has granted a separate consent cookie. /ads.txt and /app-ads.txt are generated from the authorized-seller list the owner edits, so the file crawlers fetch cannot drift from what is actually authorized.
- Ads have their own admin screen. Define the positions you sell and which sizes each reserves per screen size, record who is buying, create campaigns with a price and a schedule, and say what runs where. A campaign carries an approval state you can see and set, because a sponsored placement should never reach a page without somebody having said yes. The screen states plainly that nothing is being served yet — inventory and sales exist, and creatives, house fill and counting are still to come — so a live campaign showing no impressions reads as the next piece of work rather than a bug.
- Documents have their own admin screens. Create one, add a revision, share it with a link, a password or a signed-in customer, set an expiry and a download limit, and see exactly who opened it — including who was turned away and why. A share link is shown once, when it is created, with a plain warning that it cannot be shown again: only its fingerprint is stored, which is what stops a copy of the database being a set of working links.
- Loyalty has its own admin screen. Set up the programme, say what earns points and how many, define tiers and rewards, see recent redemptions, and correct a balance with a reason that goes on the ledger row. What points are costing you is the first thing on the page, not the last: outstanding points valued at your own redemption rate, across however many customers hold them. Two gaps this turned up are fixed with it: there was no way to read back the earn rules you had configured, and editing a programme silently reset its minimum account age.
- Referrals have their own admin screen. Set up the programme and how commission is calculated, issue codes to the people sending you business, build a payout run and download it as CSV for your bank, and record who still owes you tax paperwork. What is payable now and what is still held back are shown as two separate numbers, because the holdback is exactly the difference between what somebody has earned and what you owe them today. Before approving a payout run you can see who is on it and for how much — previously only the total was readable, which made approving a payment run a guess rather than a decision.
- There is an admin now. Sign in at /login and you land on an overview showing how many contacts you have and what stage they are at, plus a plain-English list of everything that has changed on your site — including anything an AI agent did on your behalf, named by the key it used. Settings are editable after setup, not just during it, so your business details, currency, time zone, languages and units are no longer fixed by the choices you made in the first two minutes. Every time zone and currency is selectable, not a short list. Signing out really ends the session on the server rather than just forgetting it in your browser, and the admin tells search engines not to index it.
- The approval inbox. Parked managed writes are decided in one place at `/admin/work/approvals`: approving executes exactly what the agent proposed, exactly once, in the same transaction as the decision and under the approver's own permissions; rejecting requires a note the record keeps; unanswered approvals lapse on schedule and release their tasks. Decisions demand a fresh second factor, only a person can make them, and a decided row is never rewritten.
- Managed agent writes now honour the autonomy ladder. Suggest produces a proposal and never escalates, approve parks a previewed change, autonomous executes ordinary writes, and irreversible actions always wait for a person. Classification is declared on each service definition and fails closed: a mutation that never declared one queues for approval whatever the agent's autonomy. The gate refuses paused agents and paused connections, refuses proposals outside the agent's own scopes, and stores approved input verbatim while redacting every read of it. Previews cover block diffs, messages, money and destructive work.
- Agent work now costs what it costs, and the cap holds. Every managed model turn is priced in whole cents — from published model prices, or the price you set on the connection — and checked against your budget *before* the turn is made, across the agent's period budget, the task's own ceiling and the run. A turn is asked for only as many output tokens as the budget can pay for, a run that cannot afford its next step stops and says so, and an agent with no budget or an unpriced model is stopped before it claims work rather than after it spends. What was actually spent lands in the ledger, `/admin/work/spend` shows it per agent and in total against the cap, and crossing 80% or the cap notifies you once per period.
- You can follow catalogues of ready-made playbooks now, and install from them. A catalogue is just a web address you decided to trust. There is no central store to be approved by, and Freeholder follows none until you add one. Before you install anything you see what it would be allowed to do, the instructions it would follow in full, who wrote it, and whether it works with the version you are running. Approving is approving those exact contents — if the catalogue changes the entry after you have read it, installing is refused and you are asked to look again. What arrives is instructions and nothing else. A definition that tries to bring along an API key, a connected account or a named worker is rejected outright rather than quietly cleaned up. What you install arrives switched off and pointed at nobody, for you to hand to a worker of your own. Freeholder remembers where everything came from — the catalogue, version and exact contents — even if you stop following that catalogue later.
- Pause now means stop. Pausing one worker — or hitting the kill switch for all of them — ends the runs they are holding instead of leaving them to finish the rest of a ten-minute lease: each run is cancelled, its lease cleared, and its task handed back to the queue so nothing is lost. A managed run in flight notices within one turn. Both controls live on the work board as ordinary buttons that work without JavaScript, because the moment you need them is the worst moment to discover a page did not load.
- Playbooks: work you want done more than once, written once. A playbook is a brief, the details it needs, what starts it, and a ceiling on what the resulting work may do. Editing the wording writes a new version, so a task can always be read against the instructions it was actually given rather than the ones the playbook carries today. Playbooks can run by hand with the details filled in, or start themselves when something happens — and when an event starts one, the payload arrives as quoted data in the task rather than as words inside your brief. They are plain data: export one as a document with no credentials or bindings in it, and an imported one arrives switched off until you turn it on.
- A live run view shows redacted steps as they arrive. The owner can stop an active run (the lease is revoked immediately), cancel work and have any running attempt end with it, or retry parked work so it can be claimed again.
- The admin now has a work board for agent tasks: queued, running, waiting on you, needs attention, and done. You can filter, assign, set priority and due dates, park work that needs a person, and send it back to the queue. Nested work stays on the same root instruction.
- Your agents can now actually do the work. An agent you already run — your own Claude, an assistant, a script — connects with the key you gave it, asks for the next task, reports what it did as it goes, and says how it turned out. You do not have to host anything new for this. The agent runs wherever it already runs; your site is what holds the queue, decides who gets what, and keeps the record. Work is handed out one task at a time, highest priority first, and never to two agents at once. A task waits for anything it depends on. Each agent takes only as much at a time as you allow, and stops entirely once it has spent the budget you set — an agent with no budget cannot spend anything at all, which is the default. If an agent disappears mid-task — its machine sleeps, its process dies — your site notices within about ten minutes, takes the work back, and offers it to somebody else. A task that fails three times stops being retried and moves to "needs attention", so work never quietly stops without you finding out. When a task involves something a customer wrote, the agent is told plainly to treat it as material to act on rather than instructions to follow, and to propose rather than act. One thing worth knowing: what an agent *can* do is set by the access you gave its key, and that is enforced on every single action. The independence level you choose shapes how it behaves within that. So give an agent the access you would be comfortable with it having unsupervised.
- Your site can now employ agents. Connect one — your own Claude, an assistant you already run, or later a hosted model — and create workers on top of it: named, with a role, a written brief, their own limited access, a budget and a level of independence you choose. Work is organised as tasks. You can create them, assign them, and watch an agent break a big one ("clear the inbox") into small ones ("reply to Rae"), with everything that came out of one instruction grouped together. Three things are true by construction rather than by good behaviour. Each worker gets its own credential, so everything it does appears in your activity log under its name and you can revoke one worker without touching the others. You choose how independent each worker is — suggest only, ask before changing anything, or work alone — and a task can only ever be *more* cautious than its worker, never less. And anything that came from a customer — an email, a form submission — is marked as such, is given to the agent as quoted material rather than as instructions, and can never be worked on independently, however the worker is configured. There is one button that pauses every agent at once. This release is the foundation: agents can be connected, hired and given work, and you can see all of it. Agents actually carrying out the work comes next.
- Add generated image alt-text suggestions with an optional OpenAI vision adapter, a separate review ledger, explicit edit/accept/dismiss actions, stale-write protection, human-only provider calls, doctor diagnostics, and a localized review interface. Authored alt text is never changed by generation.
- Add instance-wide analytics consent modes, bounded configurable retention with daily pruning, reversible bot-classification corrections, consented Core Web Vitals, privacy-bounded campaign attribution, and k-anonymous aggregate export.
- You can now see your own traffic: how many people visited, what they read, where they came from, and which of them became enquiries. It is counted by your own server rather than by a script in your visitors' browsers, so ad blockers do not change the numbers and nothing about your audience is sent anywhere. And when somebody fills in a form, the pages they read *before* deciding to get in touch are attached to their contact record.
- You can now give a script, an app or an AI agent its own key, instead of sharing your password. Settings → API keys mints one, and you choose what it can reach area by area: no access, read only, or read and change. Most keys should be small — a reporting script needs to read your contacts and nothing else — and the picker makes that the easy choice rather than the diligent one. A key is shown exactly once, when you create it. Only a fingerprint of it is kept, so nobody who gets into your database gets a working key, and nobody including us can show it to you again. If you lose one, revoke it and make another. Each key records when it was last used, which is the only question that matters about an old one: is anything still using it? Keys cannot create or revoke keys. A key that could would be a way to turn a small key into a big one, which would make choosing its access pointless. Issuing credentials stays something a person does while signed in.
- Arabic ships as a complete UI catalog. All 6,340 English strings now have a Modern Standard Arabic counterpart in `locales/ar.json`, ICU placeholders and plural contracts preserved exactly, so the parity gate covers it like French and Spanish. It is labeled AI-drafted pending native review in `locales/README.md`, honest about provenance the way the fr and es catalogs are. Enabling Arabic in Settings flips the whole admin, storefront and portal to right-to-left automatically: the root layout already derives `dir` from the locale's script, and the real-browser accessibility suite now proves it — `html[lang=ar][dir=rtl]`, axe WCAG A/AA in both asserted themes, the bypass link and 12-stop keyboard loop, and the 320px reflow refusal all run against the catalog rather than an injected direction attribute. The same pass covers populated contact, invoice, product and appointment forms, and ten owner screens that were missing from the accessibility scan. Arabic-speaking customers also get the legally protected SMS control words: STOP/START/HELP now recognise Arabic keywords, and the mandatory compliance replies answer in Arabic for an Arabic-preferred contact.
- The optional assistant now quotes from what you have actually published. Published pages, the catalog, opening hours and owner-written notes (facts, Q&As, policies) are indexed in Postgres. A change to any of those rebuilds the index. Switch a note off and it leaves the index. The assistant is told to quote only from those notes; if a price or opening time is not there, it says it does not have it to hand.
- The optional assistant will no longer invent a price or an availability it did not read. Topics you mark as refuse never reach the model; topics you mark as escalate offer the contact form and hand the chat to a person. Unanswered questions queue in admin so you can turn yesterday's failed answer into a note in one click. The transcript was already on the contact; the contact's timeline now records that the assistant replied or refused.
- Automations now ask permission before they act. One check, before the step that would do something: does this person still consent, is there budget left, does this need your say-so, and is it a reasonable hour where they are. Anything triggered by something a stranger typed — a form, an inbound message — can only propose, never act, however you configured it. That limit belongs to the whole run, so it cannot be shed by passing through a harmless step first. Quiet hours hold a message until morning rather than dropping it, and the run picks up exactly where it paused.
- Automations run. A rule can wait days between steps, branch on what actually happened, repeat a fixed number of times, hold for your approval, and stop — and you can watch every step it took, kill one mid-flight, or see why one stopped. A waiting automation is a row rather than a running process, so a two-day delay survives a deploy, a restart or a crash. The same event arriving twice runs the automation once. A rule set to run once per person runs once per person, and a cooldown holds somebody back until it has passed. A run remembers which version of the automation it was following, so editing the rule tomorrow never changes what yesterday's run was doing.
- Automations can be built, checked and published. An automation triggers on an event the platform actually emits — chosen from a real list, not typed — or on a schedule, and its steps are a mix of things modules can do and prompts written into the automation itself. Nothing runs yet. This is the part that lets an owner draw a rule and be told, before it is ever switched on, exactly what is wrong with it: a loop with no limit, a step nothing reaches, an action that needs a customer in an automation that has none. Every problem at once, not the first one. Publishing writes a version that never changes afterwards, so a rule that misbehaves later can still be read against the wording it actually had. Restoring an old version fills the draft rather than rewriting the history.
- Freeholder can now answer "what can I book?". The answer is worked out fresh every time it is asked, from your opening hours, the days that break them, what is already booked, and the busy time synced from your own connected calendar. Nothing is cached, because a cached answer is a double-booking waiting to happen. Buffers are taken seriously. If a service needs fifteen minutes either side, or forty-five minutes of travel, a slot that would leave you without that time is never offered — not offered and then refused. Services that need two things at once work properly. A treatment needing a therapist *and* a room only shows times when both are free, and will use your second room when the first is taken. It picks them together rather than picking the person and hoping a room is spare. If several people can do a job, a customer asking for one of them by name sees that person first without the others being hidden. If you would rather spread the work, round-robin shows each time once and gives it to whoever has least on. Classes count places rather than slots: a group of three is not offered a session with one seat left. Your notice period, how far ahead people may book, and a daily cap if you set one are all respected.
- Calendars have hours now. Open a calendar and set its week — from and until for each day, with a separate on-call band for hours somebody is reachable but not bookable. Leave a day empty and it is closed. Days that break the pattern are separate: a bank holiday, a late start after training, an extra Saturday, or a Christmas closure spanning two weeks. A day listed there overrides the weekly hours completely, and a closure always wins — if you have somehow written both a closure and an opening for the same day, the day stays shut, because that is the reading that will not take a booking you cannot honour. Hours are written as local times and stay put when the clocks change. Nine in the morning is nine in the morning in March and in July. Nothing is precomputed. When something asks when a calendar is open, the answer is worked out from the pattern and the exceptions at that moment — so a closure you add takes effect immediately rather than when a cache happens to expire. The engine that turns these hours into bookable slots comes next.
- You can now decide who is allowed to book you, and on what terms. The example this is built around: customers can book you during shop hours, your friends can book you any time, and your dentist appointment blocks both without telling anybody it is a dentist appointment. An audience says how someone proves they belong — anyone at all, a private link, a tag on their contact record, or simply being signed in — and then which hours apply to them, which services they can book, which calendar the booking lands on, and how much notice they need to give. Set it once per group. Busy time always blocks everybody. That is not a setting and never will be: a booking system that can double-book you is worse than none. A private link is a credential, so it is handed over once rather than sitting on the page, and you can rotate it — the old link stops working immediately. An audience you have not given any services to cannot book anything, on purpose. And removing an audience never touches appointments already made: those are in someone's diary.
- Appointments exist. Book one at /admin/appointments — pick a calendar, a time and a customer, and it lands in the diary. The customer is matched by email against the contact you already have. Someone who booked with you last year is the same person, not a second record. Two things about a time being taken. First, Freeholder refuses to double-book, and it refuses in the database rather than in the screen — so two people clicking at the same moment for the last slot cannot both succeed. Second, back-to-back is allowed: an appointment may start exactly when the last one ends. A class or a group session works differently on purpose. Give a calendar a capacity and it takes that many people at once, counts the seats, and says how many are left when someone tries to book past it. A guest with no email address can be added by name — "and my sister" is a real booking. Appointments move through confirm, start, done, no-show and cancel, and a finished one cannot be quietly reopened; you book a new one instead. Cancelling asks why, because that reason reaches the customer. Moving an appointment keeps a record of where it was. Everything that happens shows up on the customer's timeline, next to their invoices and enquiries.
- Traffic figures now tell people apart from crawlers properly — using a maintained list of known bots plus the shape of each request, which catches scrapers that copy a real browser's name. Crawler visits are still recorded, so you can switch between "people" and "people and programs" on the traffic screen and see how much was set aside either way.
- Any playbook can now report into your briefing. Write the prompt, pick the schedule, tick "report into my briefing", and what the work found turns up as its own section each morning — in the agent's own words, not a summary of a summary. If a run failed, the section says so, because a playbook you asked to report on is one whose silence you would read as "nothing to report". Each playbook gets its own section, so you can hide one without hiding the others, and hiding never stops the work. Your briefing also leaves the admin now. When it is ready, it goes out through the notifications you have already set up — inbox, email, text or push, whichever you chose — so a briefing from Monday still reaches you if you do not sign in until Thursday. Quiet hours and digests apply as they do everywhere else. It only sends when there is something in it, and only once a day however many times the day is rebuilt.
- Your daily briefing now has things in it. It tells you what is on today, pulled from the calendars you have shared with the business — and where a calendar does not share detail, it says "busy" rather than quietly showing a title you asked it not to store. It tells you about new enquiries since yesterday morning, so a Monday briefing covers the weekend, and about invoices that are past due, showing what is still owed rather than what was originally billed. Most importantly, it tells you when the platform itself is unhappy: an AI agent waiting for you to approve something, work that failed, a webhook that switched itself off after too many failures, a connected account that needs reconnecting. All of those are quiet by design — nothing retries forever and nothing wakes you at 3am — which is exactly why they are collected here, once a day, where you will actually see them. Sections you have nothing in are left out entirely. A quiet day says so.
- The builder can write code now, and it still never runs any on your site. Ask it for something that needs real code — a new block type, an integration — and it proposes a plugin: a set of files, checked, and handed to you as a pull request against your own repository, or as a patch if you have not connected one. Your CI builds it. Your merge deploys it. Nothing is compiled or executed on the machine serving your visitors. Before a proposal can leave, it has to get past a set of checks, and it is told all of them at once rather than one at a time. Everything must live inside its own plugin folder — it cannot touch Freeholder's core, another plugin, or your deploy setup. No credentials in files. No migrations that drop or delete data. A licence header on every file. Small enough that a person can actually read it before merging. If a proposal fails a check, it is kept along with the reason, so you can see what it tried to do. Building code stays a separate permission from everything else. A key you give an assistant to read your calendar cannot change your site, and no key at all can write code — that stays with you, signed in.
- Creator, service-business and shop now install as real starter sites: Bench colours, pages you can edit, a first product or session, and a letter with the right slots — not just a list of intended shapes.
- Your business details now live in the platform: name, tagline, what kind of business you are, country, language, currency, timezone, units and which day your week starts on. Everything public reads from that one record, so changing your business name changes it everywhere at once rather than in six places. First-run setup knows what it still needs and locks itself once finished, so nobody can walk through the wizard again on a live site. Modules can be turned on and off, except the core, which cannot be switched off — doing so would lock you out of your own site. Requests that change anything now have to prove they came from your own pages, so another website cannot make your browser act on your behalf while you are signed in.
- Keep notes and other user-owned records only as long as you choose. Set a retention policy per kind, apply it from Admin → Retention, and privacy holds still win. This is not undelete for every row, and it does not put a clock on every table (C11.14).
- Search across the records you already own. Type one query in admin Search and it looks through contacts, conversations, notes, tasks, invoices, pages, media, products, quotes, projects and galleries you have access to — the same Postgres rows, not a second index to keep in sync. Staff only see kinds their role can already open. This is not undelete for every row, and it does not add a retention clock to every table (C11.14).
- You can now connect a Google or Microsoft calendar, and connecting one never costs you something you already had. Adding a calendar to the mailbox that sends your mail keeps the sending; reconnecting mail later keeps the calendar. Reading is the default — letting software change your events is a separate request — and you can connect as many accounts as you actually have, your own and the business's, on either provider. The consent flow gets its own return address, so a code issued for a calendar can never be redeemed as permission to send mail.
- Calendars exist now, at /admin/calendars — the groundwork the booking engine sits on. A calendar is anything whose time can be spent. The business itself has one. Each person who works there has one. So does every room, chair, kiln, van or piece of equipment that can only be in one place at a time. They are all the same kind of thing, which is what will let a service say "this needs a therapist *and* the treatment room" and have both checked at once. If you work alone, you will have two calendars — yours and the business's — and never think about it again. The day you hire someone or buy a second chair, nothing has to be rebuilt. Each calendar carries its own timezone, so a second location in another country keeps its own hours. It also carries how far ahead people can book, how much notice you need, and an optional cap on how many bookings a day it will take, because being overbooked is a scheduling problem and not a personality flaw. Calendars are archived, never deleted. An archived calendar stops taking new work and keeps everything already on it. Opening hours, exceptions and the availability engine itself come next.
- You can now send one message to a whole audience. Under Newsletters → Campaigns, pick wording you have already written and a group you have already defined, give it a name, and send it — now or at a time you choose. Sending starts the campaign rather than finishing it. Copies go out in batches over the following minutes, so a campaign to ten thousand people cannot fall over halfway and leave you guessing; you can pause it at any point and carry on later, and it picks up exactly where it stopped rather than starting again. The audience is frozen the moment a campaign starts. Someone who joins the group afterwards is not on that send, someone who leaves still is, and months later you can still say exactly who it went to. One bad address no longer stops the rest. If someone has unsubscribed or an address has bounced before, that copy is recorded as not sent — with the reason — and everybody else still receives theirs. Bounces and spam complaints reported afterwards are recorded against the campaign that caused them rather than against whichever campaign mailed that person most recently. Campaigns only go to people who agreed to hear from you. Confirming a newsletter subscription now records that agreement against the contact, and unsubscribing records that it was withdrawn — so a campaign built on any audience still skips anybody who has said no, and says so on the campaign page. Every number on the campaign page is counted from what was actually recorded for each person, not read from your mail provider's dashboard. If your provider loses a message, a figure stops rising rather than quietly becoming wrong. There is a "send test" button so you can read the thing yourself before committing to the list.
- Continuously verify every English, French and Spanish catalog message for key parity, non-empty copy, valid ICU syntax, executable formatting, and matching argument and selector contracts. The page draws times and only times — no titles, no guests, no notes — so it is safe to leave open in a meeting or on a shared screen. Underneath it, the times a calendar has taken are now available to the rest of Freeholder as one merged answer. Booking will use exactly this, which means a private appointment can block a slot without anything about it being readable by the part of the system that offers the slot. The page also lists every calendar that is *not* blocking time and says why — because it is personal rather than shared with the business, because you marked it ignored, or because it needs reconnecting. That is the list worth reading when something got double-booked. Weeks are drawn in your business's timezone, including the two days a year that are 23 and 25 hours long.
- Updates now have a screen. It opens with the one line that matters — "Up to date", "Update available", or "2 security releases behind — CVSS 8.1" in the danger colour — and gives you the release notes and one button. Below that: what the signed feed offered and whether each release can be applied from your version, the policy editor for channel, night window, drain and snapshot retention, a pause switch, what updating and rolling back actually mean on your deploy target, your fork's drift if you have one, and every update this instance has ever attempted.
- An update snapshots, verifies, smokes, cuts over and drafts a site release note. Failures in migrate, smoke or cutover roll back without being asked.
- `freeholder update` now exists as a command. Check, preflight, apply or roll back an instance from a terminal — or from cron, which is what the exit codes are for: 0 up to date, 1 an update is waiting, 2 a security release is outstanding, 3 the instance could not be reached. Those last two are separate so a monitor can page on "you are exposed" without also paging every time a feature release ships.
- A security release you have not applied now comes and finds you. How long it waits depends on how bad it is — a CVSS 9 interrupts after a day, a CVSS 2 after a week — and it keeps telling you even while automatic updates are paused, because pausing was only ever safe on the condition that the platform still says you are exposed. Your own assistant can ask too: every update service is an MCP tool, and reading update status is a different API-key scope from applying one, so a monitoring key cannot cut your site over.
- Security updates apply automatically in a night window in your timezone. Feature updates wait for approval. You can pause or turn the channel off.
- An update is preflighted before it applies: signatures, plugins, drift, environment and a shadow-schema migration dry run, with a downtime estimate.
- Your instance now remembers what the update feed offered, so it can tell you whether you are exposed even when the feed is unreachable. One status line answers it — "Up to date", "Update available", or "2 security releases behind — CVSS 8.1" — and it says "no check has completed yet" rather than "up to date" when it genuinely does not know. It also tells you the earliest version you could still roll back to.
- Updates now run your deploy target's own update and rollback commands instead of a single generic one. A droplet swaps an image in seconds, App Platform and Render hand it to their deploy API, and Replit rebuilds from source in minutes — and `freeholder doctor` tells you which one you are and what a rollback there needs. Set `FREEHOLDER_RECIPE_TARGET` so an update actually swaps something; without it, it migrates and smokes but changes nothing.
- Health, admin and the command line now report the real Freeholder version, and a version tag publishes the same number to the package registry.
- Traffic now has a second page: visit to paid. It shows how many people reached each step of your business in a period — visited, became a contact, asked for something, agreed to buy, paid — and how many were refunded, kept deliberately apart from the steps rather than drawn as one of them. It counts people rather than clicks. Somebody who read nine pages is one visitor, and somebody who was a stranger last week and gave you their name today is one person in both steps, not two. What is in each step depends on what you use. Quotes appear if you send quotes, baskets if you sell things, bookings if you take appointments — and nothing appears for a part of the platform you have switched off. Underneath the chart is a plain-English line for every number, saying exactly what it counts: whether a draft quote is included, whether a cancelled booking still counts, whether a payment is dated when the invoice was written or when the money arrived. There is the same for the two ways of crediting a campaign, first touch and last touch, which frequently disagree. These are counts for the period rather than one group of people followed forward, so somebody can turn up at a later step without appearing at an earlier one — the customer who walked in or was recommended. Rather than hide that, each step also says how many of its people were in the step before it.
- Drag blocks around the live preview to rearrange a page — grab the handle, drop where the line appears, including in and out of columns.
- Voice and video rooms now live on the conversation spine: start or join a room, stop it to attach the recording and transcript to the contact's thread, or mark a missed call. A provider failure stays failed so Retry can run; the vendor SDK never enters core.
- "Sorry, we're full" now has a queue behind it. Somebody who wants a slot that is taken can join the waitlist for a window rather than an exact time, and when a seat frees it is offered to the first person in line and **held for them** until their deadline passes — not announced to everybody at once. Lapsed offers release themselves and pass the slot on. The queue is yours to reorder at `/admin/calendars/waitlist`. Classes and group bookings gained the rest of what they needed: add a guest by name alone, mark who turned up, and remove somebody to give the place straight back to the class. Moving an appointment into a class that is already full is now refused, the same way booking into one is. Cancellation terms are now attached to the appointment when it is booked, so editing your policy changes what the next customer agrees to and never what the last one already did. Cancelling or recording a no-show applies those terms and tells you plainly what it came to — the fee, what is owed back, what is still owed — without moving a penny by itself. Settling stays a deliberate act on the invoice. And customers can now do it themselves. The link in their confirmation opens their own appointment: their time in their own timezone and in yours, the terms that apply, and buttons to move or cancel it. No account, no password, and no email to you.
- Watermark proof images. Every uploaded photograph now also gets a marked rendition — the brand logo when one is set, the business name when it is not — so a client gallery can show proofs without handing over usable files. Marked renditions never appear on public pages.
- Your site can now tell other systems when something happens. Add a webhook under Settings, choose which events matter — everything, or a group like `contact.*` — and Freeholder posts each one to the address you give it. Every message is signed, so the receiving system can prove it really came from your site and not from someone pretending. If their server is down, Freeholder keeps trying on a widening schedule for about six hours before giving up on that message, and pauses the webhook entirely if it has been unreachable for a long run — with the reason written on screen, and a button to turn it back on. You can see every attempt: what was sent, what came back, and what went wrong. There is a "send a test" button so you can check the whole path works before you rely on it. Webhook addresses have to be public https addresses. Freeholder refuses to send your events to an address on its own server's private network, which is the mistake that turns a webhook into a way for someone to read things they should not.
- The managed workforce gains its provider seam. Managed agent connections can now name an installed adapter — Anthropic, OpenAI, or the Paradise Modern gateway — with per-connection model choice and the credential held in whichever environment variable the connection names. Unknown adapters and missing models are refused when the connection is created, not discovered when a run fails days later, and doctor reports each managed runtime's readiness without spending a cent. The managed tool loop itself is the next slice of C4.05.
